10 years after the Sagamihara stabbings, justice has still not been served to the victims

By Sanjana Ajjampur

Please be advised that this article discusses a mass murder event motivated by ableism.
A mourner pays respects at a display for victims of the Sagamihara Stabbings in 2024.

In 2023, Serigaya Lily Garden, a group home for intellectually disabled people opened in Kanagawa, Japan, on the grounds where another group home, Tsukui Lily Garden once stood.

Tsukui Lily Garden was demolished after 10 women and 9 men were murdered, and 26 people were injured in 2016, during one of the deadliest mass stabbings in history.

This story has been told many times around the world in excruciating detail, and this article need not join them.

We know that on July 26th, 2016, Satoshi Uematsu, an ex-employee of Tsukui Lily Garden, was responsible. Why is he named when the victims are not?

Killing in the Name of Care

The murderer justified his killings as an act of charity as “disabled people only cause misfortune”. As people unable to work to support themselves, his eugenic lens viewed them as a burden on society that could only be granted mercy through death.

When benefit to the collective is the priority, societal worth becomes contingent on contributions of labour. Without the perceived ability to work, participation in society becomes difficult, and one becomes ostracised from both society and personhood. Their complex existences transform into a single-note semi-citizenship, and they are denied agency in the name of the greater collective benefit. They are branded as burdens, able to be erased in a flash.

Dignity in Denial

Stating an aim to protect the surviving families due to the sensitive nature of the attack taking place at a group home for those with intellectual disabilities, the Kanagawa police did not release the names of the victims when they typically would.

Many families do not want to share the names of those who died, to avoid discrimination, to mourn in privacy, or to separate themselves from the incident and the people.

「Even today, the victims remain nameless and faceless. Their dignity is lost.1」

Osamu Nagase and other disability rights scholars criticised the decision, fearing that erasing their names further deepens the divide between disability and personhood. With their names, disability is hidden away from broader societal view, maintaining the ideal homogenous collective and emphasising the “other”. Though anonymity aimed to protect families from discrimination, its reinforcement of difference furthers the discrimination itself.

A memorial evermore

Though their names remain erased, the love for them does not fade. Memorials continue to be held, with ex-employees of the group home fondly remembering those who passed.

「One of the victims was a man like a leader, who had looked after the employees and helped them learn their jobs.2」

Through their memories, the 19 victims are cherished as more than victim numbers. They record every memory as proof that they have lived.

「…Another man loved trains and would demonstrate his close imitation of the conductors’ voice, saying, “The doors are now closing.”2」

In Kobe, a disability activist group continues “protesting against disabled discrimination and eugenic ideology” through silent protests each year in their name.

Disability remains taboo, and disability group homes remain viewed as places where people without personhood are “othered” away from society. But the fight to recognise their dignity will never cease as long as their memory lives on.
