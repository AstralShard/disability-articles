Japanese disability rights issues don’t end with hiring quotas. Citizenship and the workplace are linked: here’s how.

By Sanjana Ajjampur

Japan’s aging population is no new revelation. Today, almost 30% of its residents are 65 years and older. So, it comes as no surprise when accessibility is promoted and implemented based on its service to older citizens over disabled people, erasing the reality that accessibility needs can differ dramatically between these groups.

Heisei Era? More like, uh, Naysay era

The Heisei Era was defined by the highest highs and the lowest lows, with the job market severely impacted by the economic bubble pop. The tumultuous job market resulted in heavy expectations for unreachable standards, especially towards young people sold a now impossible dream by its beneficiaries. In a helpful diagram below:

For a long time, university was considered the gateway to adulthood, with employment seeming like a guarantee during the economic bubble.

But what happens when it’s not so easy? Without a stable job, your labour cannot regularly contribute to society, and monetary support no longer seems to provide returns. In an environment where benefit to the collective is prioritised above all else, your participation in society is gated, and you are branded with a form of semi-citizenship.

「…unable “to fit” into the secure and routinized pathways traced by older generations…in all of these areas the old criteria of “success”, “achievement”, or “happiness” have become outdated if not obsolete.1」

If the ability to work to an acceptable standard is the key to societal participation, barriers to work become barriers to society. The pressure to succeed under threat of ostracisation is only worsened when you bring disability into the mix. Due to different accessibility needs, disabled people are viewed as universally unable to work at the same degree as abled people, and hiring practices reflect that.

Government and Corporate (In)action

These issues have not gone unnoticed, even in an official government capacity. Labour shortages have provided a favourable environment for more diverse hiring practices, so legislation pushes for increased disability representation in a shrinking workforce.

Employers with more than 100 regular workers must have 2.5% of their workforce consist of disabled people, with benefits for exceeding and fines for not meeting these guidelines. But accommodations require funds for less efficient workers, steering companies away from perceived liability.

「If I mention [disability] in an interview, it’s harder for me to get hired.2」
Yukiko Sakamoto, who served under the MLHW (2004-2009), aimed to crack down on companies who did not abide by disability hiring guidelines. Many firms today still do not reach these quotas.

However, hiring alone is not the pinnacle of inclusivity. Disabled worker retention is lowered by inadequate accommodations and hostile perceptions.

The Ouroboros of Ostracism

「I’m worried that asking for accommodations might result in communication issues or cause me to be treated unfavourably.2」

If accommodation requests are consistently met with disgruntlement, refusal, or mistreatment, one message becomes clear: that disability is yours to deal with alone.

Self-sacrifice becomes requisite to employment, and disability becomes medicalised as a personal failing for differing from the norm, further contributing to the unfavourable treatment that bars them from employment.

【survey】25% of Japan firms have failed to accommodate workers with disabilities

Any inability to work for the collective becomes the responsibility of the individual over the system, and from tokenistic action and limited consideration, governing bodies and companies are absolved from striving to improve working and living conditions for disabled people.

Once more, disability proves a pathway to semi-citizenship, and the ouroboros eats its own tail.

Wait, it’s all the social model? Always has been/The illusion of choice/a secret third, more sinister thing/Damned if you do, damned if you don’t

With societal integration contingent on work output, the idea of a “cure” is alluring on a cultural level. “Rehabilitation” becomes institutionally emphasised, a phenomenon often critiqued by Japanese disablity activists like Shinya Tateiwa. It can “cure” any outward appearance of deficit, enabling a normal life with normative societal contribution.

It is a path to the ultimate selfless goal: integrating into the collective, serving the community above oneself, and giving without taking. Why burden society when you can burden yourself? Any acknowledgement of differences becomes impolite, disturbing the status quo. Any responsibility to accommodate different citizens just disturbs community harmony.

When disability cannot be sufficiently ignored, a certain duality becomes apparent. Someone unable to keep up with the hardships of life is not afforded agency. Someone able to compensate through disproportionate, damaging effort, is crowned with invisibility. The workplace is merely one avenue through which this dichotomy is enforced.

Disability is more than just individual impairment. Societal views contribute to barriers throughout life, be they to accommodations, work, or citizenship itself. Without true personhood, disabled people will not be consulted enough on their own lives, legislation, and accommodation, and the cycle continues evermore.

Disability cannot truly belong in this form of citizenship without societal change.
